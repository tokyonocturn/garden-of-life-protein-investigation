# 09. Manufacturing, regulation, recalls, lawsuits and corporate ownership

## 9.1 Who makes it

Garden of Life LLC is headquartered in **Palm Beach Gardens, Florida**. Since **March 2018** it has been a wholly owned subsidiary of **Nestlé Health Science**, itself part of Nestlé S.A., following Nestlé's **US$2.3 billion** acquisition of Atrium Innovations (announced 5 December 2017, closed 1 March 2018). Atrium's largest brand was Garden of Life; its other brands include Pure Encapsulations, Douglas Laboratories, Genestra and Klean Athlete.

Nestlé Health Science also acquired a **majority stake in Orgain** in 2022. Orgain is one of the most direct organic plant protein competitors and is frequently compared with Garden of Life in reviews. Two brands that compete on "independent organic" positioning share an owner.

Manufacturing: Atrium had seven factories in the US, Canada, Europe and Argentina at the time of acquisition. Garden of Life products are labeled "Made in the U.S.A. from foods grown in the U.S.A. and other countries", with facility allergen statements indicating shared lines. The company does not publish a plant-by-plant manufacturing map for each SKU.

## 9.2 The regulatory framework

Dietary supplements in the US are regulated as a **category of food**, not as drugs:

- **DSHEA (1994)** created the framework: no pre-market approval, no requirement to prove efficacy, and structure/function claims are allowed without FDA review provided the label carries the disclaimer "These statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."
- **21 CFR 111 (cGMP)** requires identity, purity, strength and composition, and requires manufacturers to establish specifications, including contaminant limits, for each component and finished product. It does **not** prescribe numeric heavy-metal limits for protein powder.
- **Adverse event reporting** is mandatory (21 CFR 111.240), but there is no public database of protein-powder AERs comparable to drugs.
- **Recalls** are generally voluntary; the FDA classifies them (Class I = reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences or death; Class II = may cause temporary or medically reversible harm; Class III = unlikely to cause harm).

The practical consequence: **Garden of Life's "tested for heavy metals" claim is a cGMP obligation, not a safety guarantee.** Every US supplement manufacturer is required to test; the question is the limit it sets and whether it publishes the result.

## 9.3 Recalls and enforcement

| Date | Product | Class | Reason | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Jan–Feb 2016** | RAW Meal Organic Shake & Meal (chocolate, original, vanilla, vanilla chai) | **Class I** | *Salmonella* Virchow | Initial recall 29 Jan 2016; expanded 12 Feb 2016; ~61 lots; 33 illnesses, 6 hospitalizations, 23 states; FDA traced to organic moringa leaf powder |
| **Sep–Oct 2017** | Baby Organic Liquid probiotic, 56 mL | **Class I** | Choking hazard from product thickness | Infant product |

| **2024** | Facility inspection | NAI | No action indicated | Routine |

The 2016 outbreak is the most instructive. It was not a heavy-metal problem or a labeling problem; it was a **supplier qualification failure** on a botanical ingredient (moringa) in a product marketed as a raw, whole-food meal replacement. The company recalled, traced the source, changed suppliers and renamed the line. That is a functioning recall system, after the failure.

## 9.4 Proposition 65: the 2015 settlement

In February 2015, As You Sow filed a 60-day notice alleging that Garden of Life products exposed consumers to **lead and cadmium** above Prop 65's MADLs. The notice covered:

- RAW Organic Protein Powder (all flavors)
- RAW Organic Fit Protein Powder (all flavors)
- RAW Organic Meal Protein Powder (all flavors)
- Whole Foods exclusive RAW Organic Protein and RAW Organic Meal
- Perfect Food RAW Organic Powder and Perfect Food Powder
- Organic Plant Protein (all flavors)

The settlement (18 March 2015) and consent judgment (26 June 2015) provided:

- **$45,000** non-contingent civil penalty
- **$85,000** attorney fees and costs
- **$45,000** payment in lieu of penalty, to As You Sow's environmental enforcement fund
- **Total: $175,000**
- **Injunctive relief: product warning and reduction in lead and cadmium**
- A **modified judgment (21 October 2015)** allowed the company **12 months** to reduce lead in covered products **or** carry a warning. A further **$35,000** was recorded.

This is the single most important legal fact in the report. It means:

1. Garden of Life has had **actual notice** of lead and cadmium exceedances in its protein line since at least 2014–2015.
2. The company committed to **reduce** the metals, not merely to label them.
3. Ten years later, Consumer Reports still measured **2.76 µg lead per serving** in SPORT, 5.6× the Prop 65 MADL. Whatever reduction occurred was not enough to clear the strictest benchmark.

## 9.5 The 2025–2026 lead class action

**DeHerrera v. Garden of Life, LLC**, No. 5:25-cv-03118 (C.D. Cal.):

- Complaint filed **19 November 2025** (reported in December 2025).
- Plaintiff: Ann-Marie DeHerrera, individually and for a California class.
- Theory: Organic Plant-Based Protein is "misbranded and falsely advertised" because it is marketed as "clean and certified" and "rigorously tested for banned substances" while containing "excessive and dangerous levels" of lead.
- Key allegation: a serving contains **2.76 µg lead**, ~**564%** of Consumer Reports' 0.5 µg/day recommended limit.
- Claims: unjust enrichment, breach of implied warranty, violation of California's Consumers Legal Remedies Act and Unfair Competition Law.
- Outcome: on **26 May 2026**, Judge John F. Walter **dismissed the First Amended Complaint with prejudice**; judgment for Garden of Life entered **2 June 2026**.

A dismissal with prejudice is a merits/pleading outcome, not a finding that the product is safe. It means the court did not accept the complaint as legally sufficient (likely on preemption, pleading specificity, or the absence of a legal duty tied to CR's non-regulatory threshold). The underlying measurement is unaffected.

## 9.6 Other litigation and corporate risk

- **Data breach class actions (2025)**: *Flick*, *Grove* and *Williams v. Garden of Life* (S.D. Fla.) allege failure to protect personal information following a 2024 breach. These are privacy cases, not product-safety cases, but they are part of the corporate risk picture.
- **False-advertising exposure generally**: the "raw", "clean", "burns fat" and "heavy metals below thresholds" claims are the kinds of statements that attract Lanham Act and state UCL challenges. No public judgment on those claims was found.

## 9.7 Transparency audit

| Item | Published? | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Full ingredient lists | Yes | But "natural flavors" and blends are undisclosed |

| Manufacturing facility per SKU | No | Facility allergen statement only |
| Recall history | Public via FDA | Not consolidated by the company |

**The transparency gap is the fairest criticism of the brand.** A company that publishes lot COAs with actual lead and cadmium numbers would make this entire report unnecessary. Garden of Life has the resources (Nestlé) and the certifications to do it. It chooses not to.

## 9.8 What the regulatory record does and does not prove

**It proves:**

- The company has a documented history of heavy-metal exceedances and a 2015 settlement obliging reduction.
- It has had a Class I pathogen recall traced to a supplier.
- Its products can pass NSF Certified for Sport and still exceed a stricter lead benchmark.
- It responds to recalls and litigation in the ordinary way.

**It does not prove:**

- That any particular tub is unsafe.
- That the company is uniquely bad, most plant protein brands have similar or worse data.
- That the 2026 dismissal vindicates the product's safety claims.
- That the 2016 pathogen problem persists, the evidence says it was fixed.

## 9.9 The Nestlé question

Does Nestlé ownership matter? Practically:

- **Resources**: a large parent can afford better supplier qualification and testing. The certifications suggest some of that is real.
- **Incentives**: a large parent also has reputational and legal exposure, which argues for caution in claims, yet the "burns fat" and "heavy metals below thresholds" language persists.
- **Brand positioning**: Garden of Life still markets as an independent, mission-driven organic brand. The "we're a small company that cares" framing is not accurate and has not been for eight years.
- **Competitor ownership**: Orgain, the closest organic competitor, is also majority-owned by Nestlé Health Science. The "independent organic vs corporate" comparison is largely illusory.

None of this changes the lab numbers. It changes how much weight to give the brand's self-description.
