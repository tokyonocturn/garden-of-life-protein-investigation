# 08 — Scientific evidence review: the claims, tested

Each claim is quoted or paraphrased from Garden of Life's current product pages, then assessed against human evidence. Status labels: **Supported**, **Partly supported**, **Unsupported**, **Misleading**, **Uncertain**.

## 8.2 Protein supplementation: what is actually established

The evidence base for protein supplements is large and consistent:

- **Resistance training + adequate protein** increases muscle mass and strength. Protein supplementation adds a small benefit **when baseline intake is below ~1.6 g/kg/day**; above that, the benefit plateaus (Morton et al., *BJSM* 2018, 49 trials).
- **Total daily protein** is the dominant variable. A powder is a convenience food that helps you reach the target, not a pharmacological agent.
- **Leucine** is the key signal; ~2–3 g per dose is the usual threshold for a maximal response in young adults, higher in older adults.
- **Plant vs animal:** the literature is mixed but converging on "similar when protein intake is adequate and leucine is sufficient". A 2021 trial found a high-protein vegan diet (~1.6 g/kg/day) matched an omnivorous diet for hypertrophy and strength over 12 weeks. A 2025 RCT found a plant protein blend and an animal protein blend produced similar gains over 12 weeks. But a 2025 *Nutrition Reviews* meta-analysis of 30 RCTs found plant protein produced **slightly lower muscle mass** (SMD −0.20), with the difference concentrated in non-soy plant proteins, and a 2026 meta-analysis of acute MPS found a **small advantage for animal protein**, particularly in adults ≥65.

**Implication for this product:** a 22 g serving with 1.8 g leucine is a reasonable but not optimal anabolic dose; 30 g of SPORT (2.5 g leucine) is better; either works if daily protein is adequate. Nothing about this product is uniquely anabolic.

## 8.3 The "weight loss" formula in Raw Organic Fit

**Green coffee bean extract (Svetol).** The proposed mechanism is chlorogenic acid inhibiting glucose absorption and modulating fat metabolism. Trials are small (typically 30–50 participants, 8–12 weeks), effects are modest (a few kilograms at most), and later trials and reviews are inconsistent. The product supplies Svetol as part of a **700 mg blend with ashwagandha**, which is not the same as the 400–800 mg extract doses used in trials — the actual Svetol dose is unknown.

**Ashwagandha.** Reasonable evidence for stress, anxiety and sleep at 300–600 mg/day; some small trials report fat loss and strength gains, but the literature is short-term and heterogeneous. Rare hepatotoxicity is documented.

**Chromium.** Decades of trials, mostly null or trivially small effects on weight and body composition. The 35–120 µg in Fit is below the 200–1,000 µg doses studied.

**Fiber and protein.** 28 g protein and 9 g fiber per 170 kcal will increase satiety in most people. That is a real effect — but it is a property of protein and fiber, not of Svetol, ashwagandha or chromium.

**Verdict:** the "weight loss" positioning is not supported by the ingredients at the doses provided. If Fit helps someone lose weight, it is because it is a filling, high-protein, high-fiber 170 kcal meal component, and because it may displace a worse snack.

## 8.4 Does organic food improve health?

There are **no RCTs** of organic food consumption and hard clinical outcomes, and none of this product. Observational cohorts (notably the French NutriNet-Santé study) report associations between higher organic food intake and lower risk of some cancers and metabolic outcomes, but these are heavily confounded by income, education, smoking, diet quality and lifestyle. The consistent, replicated finding is **lower pesticide residue exposure** in people who eat organic — a real benefit whose clinical significance is debated.

**For this product specifically:** organic certification does not improve protein quality, does not reduce heavy metals (it is associated with higher levels), and does not make the additives healthier. The health case for choosing it rests on pesticide avoidance and environmental/animal-welfare preferences, not on personal health outcomes.

## 8.5 Long-term consumption

There are no long-term trials of Garden of Life protein powder. What can be said:

- **Protein itself** at 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day is safe in healthy adults; higher habitual intake is not associated with faster kidney function decline in community cohorts, and the German Nutrition Society umbrella review found no evidence that high protein intake triggers kidney stones or kidney disease. CKD is the exception.
- **Heavy metals** are the long-term issue. Lead and cadmium accumulate; the risk is cumulative and dose-dependent. A serving a day at 2.76 µg lead is a small addition to background exposure, but it is an addition that a whey protein or a lower-metal plant protein would not make.
- **Erythritol** at 2–5 g/day is far below the doses in the platelet studies, but the long-term cardiovascular signal is unresolved.
- **Protein powder displacing whole foods** is a real risk in the "meal replacement" positioning: 20 g protein, 18 vitamins and 44 "superfoods" is not equivalent to a meal.

**Bottom line:** no evidence of harm from the protein; a plausible small cumulative risk from the metals; no evidence of long-term benefit beyond convenience and protein delivery.

## 8.6 What the marketing gets right

To be fair to the product, several claims hold up:

- The protein content is accurate.
- The amino acid profile is genuinely complete and better than single-source rice or hemp.
- The products are low in sugar and, for Meal and Fit, high in fiber.
- The certifications are real and independently verifiable (USDA, Non-GMO, NSF, Informed Choice, Kosher, vegan, gluten-free).
- NSF Certified for Sport on the SPORT line is a meaningful, non-trivial certification for athletes.
- There are no artificial colors, artificial sweeteners, or controversial emulsifiers like polysorbate 80 or carrageenan.
- The 2016 pathogen problem was fixed at the supplier level and there has been no repeat.

That is a better record than many competitors. The problem is not that the product is bad; it is that the **premium price and the "clean" positioning are not matched by the contaminant data**, and several of the specific claims are not true.
