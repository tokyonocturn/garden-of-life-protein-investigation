# 05. What "organic" actually guarantees

## 5.1 The legal definition

USDA Organic is a **production-process standard**, administered by the National Organic Program (NOP) within the Agricultural Marketing Service. For crops, the standards require:

- No prohibited substances applied to the land for **at least 3 years** before harvest.
- Fertility managed through tillage, crop rotation, cover crops, and allowed animal/crop wastes and synthetic materials on the National List.
- Pests, weeds and disease controlled primarily by physical, mechanical and biological methods; approved biological, botanical or synthetic substances only when those fail.
- **No genetic engineering, no ionizing radiation, no sewage sludge.**
- Organic seeds and planting stock when commercially available.
- Buffer zones and prevention of commingling with non-organic crops.

For a processed product to carry the USDA seal and be called "organic", **at least 95% of ingredients by weight must be certified organic** (excluding salt and water). Products at 70–94% can say "made with organic ingredients" but cannot use the seal.

## 5.2 The sentence that matters most

The USDA's own guidance for certifiers and producers is explicit:

> "Organic certification addresses the process involved in producing and handling a product. **Organic certification is a process claim, not a product claim** … Organic certification does not guarantee that the product is completely free of all pesticide residues or genetically modified organism (GMO) contamination."

That single sentence disposes of most of the marketing inference. "Certified organic" tells you **how the crop was grown**, not what is in the tub. It says nothing about:

- lead, cadmium, arsenic or mercury;
- PFAS, phthalates or packaging migrants;
- microbial contamination;
- protein quality or amino acid score;
- whether the product is a good value, or whether its health claims are true.

## 5.3 Pesticides: better, not zero

Organic farming prohibits most synthetic pesticides, and residue testing consistently finds **lower** pesticide residues in organic food. But:

- Some pesticides are permitted in organic production (e.g. copper compounds, spinosad, *Bacillus thuringiensis*, certain botanicals).
- Drift and commingling occur. NOP's instruction on pesticide-residue testing sets a **0.01 ppm** trigger for investigating a prohibited residue, i.e. the standard anticipates detectable residues.
- NC State Extension summarises the rule as prohibiting residues of prohibited substances **exceeding 5% of the EPA tolerance**, not any residue at all.

Mamavation's 2024 test of 11 chocolate protein powders found **one USDA organic brand with detectable glyphosate (42 ppb)** and **two organic brands with other detectable pesticides**, out of 11 products. That is a small sample, but it demonstrates the principle: the seal is not a zero-residue certificate.

**Garden of Life specifically:** its plant proteins are USDA Organic and Non-GMO Project Verified, and both certifications appear to be genuine and current. In Mamavation's test, the Garden of Life Creamy Oatmilk Chocolate Brownie had **no detectable glyphosate** but **did have propiconazole at 0.016 ppm**, a fungicide, in an organic product. Whether that reflects drift, a supplier issue, or a lab artifact is unknown, but it is the kind of result that the word "organic" leads consumers not to expect.

## 5.4 Heavy metals: organic can be worse

This is the central paradox and the most important section of this report.

Clean Label Project's 2025 study of 160 products found:

- **Organic protein powders averaged about 3× the lead and 2× the cadmium of non-organic powders.**
- **79% of organic protein powders exceeded Prop 65's lead level**, versus 28% of whey-based products.
- **77% of plant-based powders exceeded it**, versus 28% of whey.
- Chocolate flavors had far more cadmium than vanilla (the report's headline figure is 110×, which is an extreme tail rather than a typical ratio).

The mechanism is not a conspiracy. It is agronomy:

1. **Plants take up metals from soil.** Cadmium and lead enter via roots; arsenic (as arsenate) enters rice via phosphate transporters; cocoa accumulates cadmium in the bean.
2. **Protein isolation concentrates the seed's protein fraction**, and with it a share of the metals.
3. **Organic systems use organic fertilizers**, manure, compost, rock phosphate, which can contain cadmium and other metals. Sewage sludge is banned, but rock phosphate is not.
4. **Rice is irrigated in flooded paddies**, which mobilise arsenic and increase its uptake.
5. **Cocoa and dark chocolate** are well-documented cadmium sources; Consumer Reports has found lead and cadmium in dark chocolate for years.

So "organic" and "plant-based" are, for heavy metals, **risk factors rather than protections**. The Clean Label Project's "Clean Sixteen", the 16 products with the lowest contaminant levels, is dominated by **whey** products. Garden of Life's own **SPORT Grass-Fed Whey Vanilla** made that list, non-detect for all four metals, while its plant SKUs did not.

## 5.5 What the other seals mean

| Seal | What it verifies | What it does **not** verify |
|---|---|---|
| **USDA Organic** | Process standard for agricultural ingredients; 95% organic content | Heavy metals, protein quality, efficacy |
| **Non-GMO Project Verified** | GMO avoidance through supplier verification and testing | Anything about contaminants or nutrition |
| **NSF Contents Certified** | Product contains what the label says, no undeclared ingredients, contaminant limits (lead ~10 µg/day) | That the level is low by stricter standards; NSF's lead line is 20× CR's level of concern |
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | ~290 banned substances, label accuracy, contaminant limits; facility inspection | Same caveat: a product can pass NSF and still carry 2.76 µg lead/serving |
| **Informed Choice / Informed Sport** | Banned-substance testing on a batch basis; listing on a public register | Heavy metals at strict levels; the public listing often shows no numbers |
| **NSF Gluten-Free** | <10 ppm gluten, facility plan | Heavy metals |
| **Certified Vegan / Kosher** | Ingredient and process compliance | Contaminants |
| **"Raw"** | Manufacturer's own processing claim, not a third-party standard | Anything |

**The crucial point about NSF Certified for Sport.** Consumer Reports' 2.76 µg/serving result was obtained on a product that carries NSF Certified for Sport. Both facts are true because the certification's lead limit (~10 µg/day) is far looser than CR's 0.5 µg/day level of concern. A certification is evidence that a testing program exists and that the product passed *that program's* thresholds. It is not evidence that the product is low in lead by a stricter standard.

The same logic applies to Garden of Life's public statement that "all products are tested for heavy metals and the levels are below any established food safety thresholds." There is **no federal threshold for lead in protein powder**, so the statement is unfalsifiable as written. The relevant comparison is to Prop 65, USP and CR's benchmark, and by those measures the Sport product does not pass the strictest one.

## 5.6 Does the organic premium buy anything real?

**Yes, for some things:**

- Lower pesticide residues on average (well replicated in the food literature).
- No synthetic fertilizer runoff in the supply chain; no sewage sludge; no irradiation; no GMOs.
- Animal-welfare standards for dairy lines.
- A paper trail: certified operations have an Organic System Plan, inspections, and residue-testing obligations.

**No, for the things this product is marketed on:**

- It does not make the protein higher quality.
- It does not make the product low in heavy metals, statistically it makes it *more* likely to be high.
- It does not make "natural flavors", erythritol or gums healthier.
- It does not validate "raw", "easily digested", "burns fat", or "supports immune health".

## 5.7 The bottom line on certification

Organic certification is a **real, audited, legally enforceable claim about farming practice**. It is not a safety certification, and it is not a nutrition certification. For a plant protein powder, the organic label is compatible with, and statistically associated with, higher lead and cadmium than conventional whey.

If your reason for buying this product is "organic therefore clean", the premise is false. If your reason is "I want to avoid synthetic pesticides and GMOs, and I accept plant-protein metal exposure", the premise is sound, and the organic seal does real work.
