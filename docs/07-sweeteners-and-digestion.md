# 07. Sweeteners, additives, digestion, allergies and side effects

## 7.1 What people actually report

The most common complaints about plant protein powders are **bloating, gas, cramping, loose stools, constipation, and an unpleasant aftertaste**. The causes are usually one or more of: fermentable fiber, sugar alcohols, legume oligosaccharides, added gums, allergen cross-contact, or simply a large bolus of protein and fiber consumed quickly.

Garden of Life's formulas contain several of these at once. This section separates what is likely to matter from what is marketing noise.

## 7.2 Erythritol (Raw Organic Protein, Creamy Oatmilk)

**Dose:** undisclosed; likely 2–5 g per serving based on typical formulations. The label lists it inside the "flavor blend", not as a separate gram amount.

**Gastrointestinal:** erythritol is the best-tolerated polyol because ~90% is absorbed unchanged and excreted in urine. The dose that causes osmotic diarrhoea in adults is around **0.66–0.80 g/kg body weight**, roughly 46–56 g for a 70 kg adult. A 2–5 g serving is nowhere near that. For most people, erythritol is not the cause of bloating.

**Cardiovascular:** the interesting and unresolved part.

- **Witkowski et al., *Nature Medicine* 2023**: in 1,157 patients undergoing cardiac risk assessment, circulating erythritol was associated with 3-year MACE; validated in US (n=2,149) and European (n=833) cohorts (highest vs lowest quartile HR 1.80 and 2.21). In vitro, erythritol enhanced platelet reactivity; in a small intervention (n=8), a 30 g oral dose raised plasma erythritol >1,000-fold for more than two days.
- **Follow-up 2024 (*ATVB*)**: 30 g erythritol, but not glucose, acutely enhanced stimulus-dependent platelet aggregation and granule release in 10 healthy volunteers.
- **EFSA 2023 re-evaluation**: concluded the evidence **does not demonstrate a causal relationship** between erythritol as a food additive and cardiovascular risk; identified diarrhoea as the most sensitive endpoint; called for further studies.
- **FDA 2024 memo**: reviewed the Witkowski paper and did not change erythritol's GRAS status.
- **ARIC 2025 and other cohorts**: higher plasma erythritol associated with heart failure, cardiovascular death and CHD, but plasma erythritol is also produced endogenously and rises with metabolic dysfunction, reverse causation and residual confounding are plausible.

**What this means for a 3 g serving:** the intervention dose was **~10× higher** than a typical serving, and the epidemiology is about *plasma* levels that reflect both intake and endogenous production. The prudent reading is **uncertain and probably small**, not "erythritol causes heart attacks". People with high cardiovascular risk who drink multiple sweetened protein shakes daily are the group where the uncertainty matters most.

**Bottom line:** not a digestive problem at these doses; a small, unresolved cardiovascular question that is not specific to Garden of Life.

## 7.3 Stevia

**Dose:** undisclosed; steviol glycoside content not stated.

**Safety:** JECFA set an ADI of **0–4 mg/kg body weight/day** (as steviol equivalents); the FDA has accepted purified steviol glycosides as GRAS. At a 70 kg adult ADI, that is 280 mg steviol equivalents/day, far above the amount in a serving of protein powder.

**Gastrointestinal:** generally none. Some people report a bitter or licorice aftertaste, especially with whole-leaf extracts. Stevia is not a FODMAP and does not feed gut bacteria.

**Caveat:** "organic stevia leaf extract" is not the same as a characterized Reb A preparation. The label does not state which glycosides or what purity, so the product cannot be compared with the studied material.

## 7.4 Fiber: the real digestive variable

| Product | Fiber/serving | Fermentability | Likely effect |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Raw Organic Meal | 7 g | High (tapioca fiber, chia, flax, legume sprouts) | Bloating/gas common in the first week; often settles |
| Raw Organic Fit | 9 g | High (tapioca, pea fiber, chia, flax) | Highest gas risk in the range |
| Creamy Oatmilk | 5–6 g | High (inulin, acacia, oat) | Gas possible; inulin is the usual culprit |
| Raw Organic Protein | 3–5 g | Moderate | Usually tolerated |
| SPORT | 3–4 g | Moderate | Usually tolerated |
| Protein & Greens | 1–3 g | Low | Best tolerated |

**Inulin tolerance:** up to **10 g/day** native inulin is well tolerated in healthy young adults; 10 g of oligofructose substantially increases symptoms. The Creamy Oatmilk's inulin dose is undisclosed but probably 2–4 g, below the threshold for most people.

**Guar gum:** 5–11.4 g/day well tolerated; 13–40 g/day causes mild-to-moderate flatulence. The amount in these products is likely under 1 g and is not a plausible cause of symptoms.

**FODMAP context:** pea protein *isolate* is low-FODMAP; whole legumes and inulin are high-FODMAP. A person with IBS should start with the flagship or SPORT (lower fiber) rather than Meal or Fit, and should treat the Creamy Oatmilk as a test.

## 7.5 Allergens

| Allergen | Where | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Sesame** | Raw Organic Protein (sesame sprout) | **Declared** ("Contains Sesame"). Sesame became a major allergen in the US under the FASTER Act (effective 2023). This is a real risk for sesame-allergic consumers and is easy to miss on a "clean" plant protein |
| **Coconut (tree nut)** | Creamy Oatmilk | **Declared** ("Contains: Tree Nuts (coconut)"). Coconut allergy is uncommon but real; FDA classifies coconut as a tree nut for labeling |
| **Soy** | None as an ingredient | "May contain traces of soy due to agricultural practices" on the Creamy line; facility handles soy |
| **Milk, egg, wheat, fish, other tree nuts** | None as ingredients | Facility statement: "Manufactured in a facility that also processes egg, milk, soy, wheat, fish, tree nuts and sesame" |
| **Gluten** | None as an ingredient | NSF Gluten-Free certified (<10 ppm); oatmilk powder raises a cross-contact question answered by that certification |

**Practical advice:** sesame allergy, avoid Raw Organic Protein entirely; SPORT does not list sesame and is a better option, but check the current label. Coconut allergy, avoid the Creamy line. Soy allergy, the facility statement is a shared-equipment warning, not a "contains" claim; most soy-allergic people tolerate shared-line products, but the decision is individual.

## 7.6 Nickel and legume sensitivity

Legumes take up **nickel** from soil. Nickel is not a declared allergen and is not tested in any public dataset for this product, but people with **systemic nickel allergy** (contact dermatitis, dyshidrotic eczema) sometimes react to legume-heavy foods and to cocoa. If you have that history, a pea/rice protein is a plausible trigger and an elimination trial is reasonable.

Similarly, people who react to **lupin or other legumes** should note that pea protein is a legume protein; cross-reactivity is uncommon but documented.

## 7.7 Histamine and "detox" reactions

There is no histamine or DAO issue specific to this product. Reports of "detox" symptoms after starting a greens/protein powder are not a pharmacological phenomenon; they are usually fiber-related gas, a new supplement's osmotic load, or unrelated.

## 7.8 Who should be cautious

| Group | Issue | Advice |
|---|---|---|
| **Children** | Lead has no safe level; product label says not intended for children; 2.76 µg/serving exceeds the FDA children's daily lead benchmark | Do not use as a children's protein supplement |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | Same lead issue, plus Prop 65's 0.5 µg/day is specifically a reproductive-toxicity level; ashwagandha in Fit is not established safe in pregnancy | Avoid, or use only under clinical advice |
| **Chronic kidney disease** | Higher protein load is contraindicated in non-dialysis CKD (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day guidance); protein supplements "may even be harmful" | Do not use without a dietitian |
| **Warfarin / vitamin K–sensitive anticoagulation** | Raw Organic Meal contains vitamin K (18–80 µg/serving depending on label) | Monitor INR; consistency matters more than avoidance |
| **IBS / SIBO** | 7–9 g fermentable fiber in Meal/Fit; inulin in Creamy | Start low; prefer flagship or SPORT |
| **Sesame allergy** | Declared sesame in Raw Organic Protein | Avoid |
| **Coconut allergy** | Declared coconut in Creamy Oatmilk | Avoid |
| **Immunocompromised** | Live spore probiotic (*B. subtilis* DE111) | Discuss with clinician; spore probiotics are generally safe but not risk-free |
| **Thyroid disease** | Ashwagandha (Fit) may affect thyroid hormones | Caution; monitor |
| **Liver disease** | Rare ashwagandha-associated liver injury reported | Avoid Fit |

## 7.9 Side-effect summary

For a healthy adult without allergies:

- **Most likely:** none, or mild gas/bloating in the first week if using Meal or Fit.
- **Less likely:** loose stools from the fiber load; bitter aftertaste from stevia; chalky texture.
- **Uncommon:** erythritol-related GI symptoms at high intakes; nickel-related eczema in nickel-sensitive people.
- **Rare but serious:** allergic reaction in sesame or coconut allergy; probiotic bacteraemia in severely immunocompromised people (theoretical, not reported for DE111).
- **Chronic:** cumulative lead and cadmium exposure, the one risk that does not announce itself.

## 7.10 The "easily digested" claim, audited

Garden of Life says the product is "easily digested" and includes enzymes and probiotics to support that. The evidence:

- Pea and rice protein isolates are more digestible than whole legumes, so the claim is plausible on the protein alone.
- The enzyme blend has no declared activity and no product-specific trial.
- The probiotic dose (250 million CFU) is below the 1–2.5 billion CFU used in trials.
- The fiber load works in the opposite direction for sensitive guts.

**Verdict:** the protein is likely easier to digest than whole legumes, but the "digestive support" story is not demonstrated for this product at these doses. If you have a sensitive gut, the flagship or SPORT is the safer starting point, and the enzymes are not why.
